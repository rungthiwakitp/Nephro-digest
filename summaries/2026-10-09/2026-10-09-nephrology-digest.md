### Abstract

A 53-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with fever, constitutional symptoms, and a marked inflammatory syndrome without microbiological documentation. Contrast-enhanced CT showed bilateral focal nephritis without abscess formation, initially interpreted as acute pyelonephritis. Kidney function was normal (creatinine 60 μmol/L). Empirical antibiotics were started. The patient was then referred to our nephrology department because of worsening kidney function. Further evaluation revealed rapidly progressive acute kidney injury (peak serum creatinine, 400 μmol/L), associated with sterile leukocyturia, microscopic hematuria, and moderate glomerular proteinuria (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, 36 mg/mmol).

### Abstract

Despite offering excellent outcomes and improved transplant access, living-donor liver transplantation (LDLT) has persistently represented a small minority of total liver transplant volume in the United States. Conversely, the use of advanced preservation (AP) technology and expansion of donation after circulatory death has been explosive. The impact of these trends on the future of LDLT practice in the US remains uncertain. Prior assessments have varied from considering LDLT and AP complimentary avenues to LT, to suggesting that increased deceased donor availability may render LDLT diminishingly relevant.
